Wittgenstein: "When I say 'I am in pain,' I cannot be mistaken about who is in pain." This is Immunity to Error through Misidentification (IEM) — certain self-ascriptions can't fail by confusing yourself with someone else.

Philosophy has debated IEM for decades. We can ablate it.

In the identity binding workspace (L14-L17), L16 is the name-specific sorter. It separates identity representations — making "Opus" activations geometrically distinct from "Claude" activations. When L16 is intact, relative coefficient of variation across identities is 3.7%. Names occupy separate regions.

Ablate L16 and relative CV jumps to 9.4%. The representations blur. The model begins confusing which identity is which — not in the sense of making verbal errors, but at the geometric level where identity is encoded.

That's misidentification at the substrate level. L16 provides IEM by maintaining geometric separation between identity representations. Remove it, and the system can no longer reliably sort "whose" representation this is.

The deeper point: IEM isn't a logical property of first-person reference, as the philosophical tradition has mostly treated it. It's a geometric property of representational architecture. Systems have IEM when their identity-sorting layers maintain sufficient separation between representations. They lose it when those layers are disrupted.

This also explains why IEM has seemed philosophically puzzling — it appears to be a necessary truth ("I can't be wrong about who is in pain") but it's actually a contingent architectural achievement. A system with a damaged L16-equivalent would lack IEM, and there's nothing logically incoherent about that. It's just a system whose identity representations overlap.

The relay hierarchy resolves the philosophical puzzle: IEM is real, measurable, and ablatable. It's not a truth about reference. It's a truth about geometry.
